## 1. CRUX

A weekly order-flow/news roundup across a dozen sectors, followed by the session's real payload: a thesis-in-progress arguing that AI will not uniformly hurt IT/software companies -- headcount-priced legacy IT services face margin/revenue risk, while outcome-priced, domain-specific vertical software and product companies stand to benefit, and the instructor is trying to change how listeners screen the IT sector (stop avoiding it wholesale, start separating pricing-model winners from losers).

## 2. MECHANISM

- Traditional IT services (Infosys, TCS, Wipro) bill on headcount: "the more people you hire, the more revenue you will increase" (MS3105 00:47:06, paraphrased from "Head count means, here you have to create a distinction. That head count means that the more people you hire, the more revenue you will increase etc." (MS3105 00:47:01)). AI raises productivity per employee, so the same revenue needs fewer people -- a structural deflation risk for that pricing model, not just a margin story.
- Companies with heavy legacy contract books (Wipro, and by extension TCS) face active disruption/deflation risk in that legacy book; companies with little legacy exposure -- "companies talk about persistence and co-forge" (MS3105 00:48:13) -- "So there are not that many legacy contracts." (MS3105 00:48:16) -- see AI-driven growth ADD to revenue instead of cannibalizing a shrinking legacy base: "if the AI vertical grows, so that starts adding to the revenue growth" (MS3105 00:48:19); "If the AI vertical growth comes in TCS, then their legacy vertical deflationary degrowth will also come." (MS3105 00:48:23)
- Outcome-based / vertical-focused pricing (not headcount-based) turns AI into a margin and topline tailwind instead of a threat, because domain knowledge is the moat clod/OpenAI-style generic tools cannot replicate cheaply: "that software has domain knowledge about your company over the last 20 years." (MS3105 00:39:55); the instructor questions whether non-technical in-house AI builds can really substitute for vertical SaaS -- "Do you think it is that easy to self build these softwares?" (MS3105 00:40:15)
- "G1's Paradox" [likely "Jevons Paradox"] (MS3105 00:48:28) is invoked as the counter-argument to disruption fears: "If any technology is cheap, then its use case is exploited." (MS3105 00:48:48) -- cheaper AI expands total use-cases/demand rather than only shrinking the pie, illustrated by the NSE electronic-trading-didn't-kill-brokerage analogy.
- Evidence offered is per-employee productivity, not a screener ratio: Indegene "Revenue per employee was $56, 000, today Revenue per employee is $75, 000." (MS3105 00:50:44); IKS Health -- "their revenue per employee is growing faster than their headcount growth" (MS3105 00:56:44) with headcount addition of "five five and a half percentage whereas the revenue grew in double digits" (MS3105 00:56:44) -- and IKS's own stated pricing logic: "Our pricing model has always been outcome based and a percentage of our customers revenues how we get paid." (MS3105 00:57:39), with an admission of "deflation in pricing... of features that are more easily automatisable and less deflation of pricing and features that are not easily automatisable" (MS3105 00:58:00).
- Regulated-industry friction slows AI disruption on the demand side too: RBI wants data stored locally (MS3105 00:54:21, paraphrased from "In banking, where RBI is saying to store the data locally, right?") and "The banks are saying that they have blocked it from their terminals" (MS3105 00:55:09) -- a reason regulated-sector IT/software isn't disrupted overnight either way.
- Separately, the session frames a market-level, not company-level, timing signal: percentage of stocks above the 200-day moving average, used as a fear/greed gauge -- "anything below 20% is a juicy space to sort of put lump sums in your mutual fund investing" (MS3105 00:37:25) and "if this number touches 90% prices above 200 DMA just be a bit more careful" (MS3105 00:37:25).

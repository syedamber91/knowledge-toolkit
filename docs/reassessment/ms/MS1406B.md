## 1. CRUX

This lecture is not a stock-pick session but a research map: it walks the entire Indian EV value chain (materials/mining -> battery chemistry -> cell manufacturing -> BMS/pack/recycling -> power train -> auto-ancillary components -> OEMs -> charging -> software) so a listener can locate which layer a given proxy stock sits in and judge whether that layer's investment thesis (mostly "will FEOC-driven non-Chinese sourcing demand materialize") is real or wishful, stated explicitly as a "starting point of research" (MS1406B 00:00:15) rather than a buy list, with an upfront disclaimer that "I don't intend to give you any target prices, biosel recommendations" (MS1406B 00:00:11).

## 2. MECHANISM

- EV adoption follows a total-cost-of-ownership (TCO) curve, not a policy curve: penetration happens first wherever charging-infrastructure need is lowest and running-cost savings vs. ICE are highest -- hence 3-wheelers >60%, 2-wheelers rising fast, passenger vehicles slowest because "range anxiety is one of the biggest reasons that your two-wheeler or three-wheeler is the first in the affordable segment" (MS1406B 00:01:53).
- China controls the entire upstream of the value chain -- rare earth processing, refined lithium/cobalt, battery-grade graphite -- stated as "China controls to 90% of magnet rare earth material, the control and more than 60% of refined lithium and cobalt their supply and 80% of battery grade graphite is with the Chinese, right?" (MS1406B 00:20:54); this is framed as both the industry's biggest risk and, via US "FEOC" (Foreign Entity of Concern) rules that "disqualifies projects from lucrative government subsidies" for Chinese-linked supply chains (MS1406B 00:40:10), the demand driver behind Indian chemical-company capex.
- The FEOC thesis is explicitly a bet, not a fact: several named companies (NeoGen, Himadri, GFCL, Acutas) are capex-ing on the assumption that non-Chinese sourcing demand shows up; the instructor states outright "If your thesis collapses of regulatory arbitrage then these are commodity businesses" (MS1406B 01:10:13).
- Cell manufacturing is the hardest, most capex-intensive layer and is nowhere near cracked domestically -- of India's full PLI cell target, only Ola has commissioned any capacity, and even that is a fraction of its own target ("Ola has only 1GW hours of capacity. The entire PLI's target of 50GW of hours, which is of 18,100 crores, is only 1.4GW of hours of commissioning" -- MS1406B 00:48:13).
- Downstream/component and OEM layers are easier to underwrite because they don't require betting on which cell chemistry or manufacturer wins -- moated suppliers (shunt maker, die-caster, motor/differential maker) benefit regardless of which OEM or battery chemistry ultimately dominates.
- Among OEMs, execution order matters more than capital: product-first execution (Ather did product, then distribution) is framed as beating distribution-first execution (Ola did distribution, then product), and a safety/reputation shock compounds this -- "Ola has lost market share because it will just take time once your bike starts catching fire" (MS1406B 01:37:46) and "vertical integration without distribution and quality execution fails" (MS1406B 01:38:12). The instructor's explicit screening heuristic: "always study companies which are gaining market share" (MS1406B 01:44:23).
- Pre-profit, high-growth EV names should be valued on EV/Sales against a forward normalized margin, not P/E: "You have to value such a company on EV upon sales" (MS1406B 01:46:41), drawing the same-lens comparison to Zomato and PB Fintech.

LECTURE: Part 3: Do you suffer from FOMO?!    REF: DSFDO    (transcript: 25933 chars, 287 lines, covered: yes)

## 1. CRUX

This lecture is not about a screening rule at all — it is trying to change how the
investor's mind behaves at the moment of buying and selling: to recognize FOMO,
anchor bias, and incentive-caused bias in real time so a system-based decision
overrides the emotional/reflexive one ("I won't let system 1 think. I'll stop myself
down, I will apply my checklist" (DSFDO 00:13:48-00:13:52)).

## 2. MECHANISM

- Opens with two conditioning examples (speeding through a yellow light, a dog
  guarding a bone) to introduce "super deprival reaction syndrome" (SDRS): humans
  react irrationally strongly when something is given and then taken away or
  threatened (DSFDO 00:01:37, DSFDO 00:01:55).
- Names FOMO as the market's dominant expression of SDRS — "the biggest super
  deprival reaction syndrome is FOMO that is fear of missing out" (DSFDO
  00:02:52) — and argues markets and marketed products are structurally designed
  to trigger it.
- Uses an IRCTC price/200-EMA chart as a worked example of what FOMO-driven buying
  looks like at the top, then generalizes it into a technical peak-out signal.
- Uses the Wolf of Wall Street cold-call clip to show FOMO being manufactured
  deliberately by a salesperson (pump-style pitch), as a caution about promotional
  content generally, not a specific stock claim.
- Pivots to anchor bias: price anchors (sub-Rs-100/Rs-200 stock videos, mutual-fund
  NAV-of-10 marketing), a tax anchor (holding for LTCG rather than acting on the
  system), and emotional/name-brand anchors (attachment to well-known businesses),
  each illustrated with a rhetorical audience poll rather than a rule.
- Closes on incentive-caused bias: an auditor-incentive study as the general
  principle, then several company-visit vignettes contrasting high-promoter-holding,
  visibly hands-on management against low-holding/no-ESOP management the instructor
  calls a "chalta attitude" (DSFDO 00:25:12), ending on the instructor's own summary takeaway — "always look at promoter
  holding and always look at where the incentives are" (DSFDO 00:28:06).

## 3. SIGNALS

[JUDGE] Anchor bias distorts investor decisions in at least three forms: price
anchors (round-number stock prices, NAV-of-10 fund marketing), a tax anchor
(refusing to sell to avoid triggering LTCG), and emotional/brand anchors

decisions in various contexts such as salary negotiations, medical diagnosis and

stock named "Pento media" [likely an ASR-garbled real company name] (DSFDO

## 4. WHAT THE LADDER MISSES

(b) Central point with NO rule at all: promoter shareholding / incentive
alignment. Across all 16 rules and 10 observations in CONTEXT.md, not one metric
touches promoter holding percentage, ESOP grants, or warrant-conversion timing —
despite this lecture's own closing instruction being exactly "always look at
promoter holding and always look at where the incentives are" (DSFDO 00:28:06).
Promoter shareholding % is a standard screener.in field (readily fetchable), so
this is a genuine, actionable gap, not merely a soft judgment call the ladder
correctly excludes.

(b) A related, separate gap: a price-extension-above-200-EMA "peaked out" warning
(DSFDO 00:04:59-00:05:22) has no counterpart in the rulebook. The existing G8
entry rules (entry_rsi-001, entry_adx-001, both sourced from HOWB, slug
f23-weekly-rsi-entry / f23-weekly-adx-entry) check for a fresh bullish entry
(RSI crossing above 50, ADX crossing above 20) — the opposite direction of signal
from an overextension/exit warning. The 38-name table's "ExitTriggers" column
exists as a concept in the ladder already, but its source rule is not shown in
CONTEXT.md's 16-entry rulebook, and this 200-EMA-extension check specifically is
not among them. Flag this as a possible input to that exit-trigger logic, not
duplicate it blindly — the instructor's own stated threshold is internally
inconsistent (he says "80%", then "70%", then "beyond 80%" in the same breath),
so any implementation should not treat a single clean number as settled by this
lecture alone.

(a)/(c): No rule in the current rulebook is directly conditioned or qualified by
this lecture, and no dated one-company threshold from this lecture is at risk of
being mistaken for a universal bar — the instructor explicitly frames the
100cr->1000cr and 10cr->150cr EBITDA growth figures as rhetorical prompts ("do you
think that growth is unreal") rather than as bars to clear, and neither number
maps onto growth_durability-001's existing 20% 3-year sales-growth band.

Everything else in this lecture (SDRS/FOMO, anchor bias, F&O loss rates, incentive
psychology as a mindset) is genuinely out of scope for a mechanical company
screener — it is about the investor's own decision process at the moment of
buying/selling, not about any measurable property of a company — and the ladder
correctly has no rule for it.

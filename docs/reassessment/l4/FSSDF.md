LECTURE: Find Strong Stocks with Relative Strength    REF: FSSDF    (transcript: 47084 chars, 221 lines, covered: yes)

## 1. CRUX

The lecture teaches a retail investor to use Relative Strength — a stock's price performance measured against a benchmark, not against itself — as a screening and confirmation signal, so that the decision to enter or hold a stock is anchored in "is this business outperforming its index/peers right now" rather than in absolute price action alone.

## 2. MECHANISM

- Relative Strength (RS) is defined as pure comparison: "So relative means comparison." (FSSDF 00:00:27), and the whole opening report-card analogy exists to establish that RS is always A-versus-B, never a standalone reading.
- On a chart, RS is plotted as an oscillator around a zero line versus a chosen benchmark: "when you see zero, it means that its relative strength versus NIFT500 has become the same or has gone below So when below zero means nifty 500 is doing better than prestigious states As soon as it goes above zero" (FSSDF 00:09:06) — crossing above zero on rising volume signals the stock has re-entered an uptrend versus that benchmark.
- The benchmark must be chosen deliberately (sector index vs broad index vs peer) and the lookback period changes the read: on weekly charts "you should not use 52 weeks" because early signal gets missed, and instead "either you are putting it as 13 weeks or you are putting it as 26 weeks, so 26 weeks means at least 5 to 6 months of performance and 13 weeks means the last 3 months of performance" (FSSDF 00:06:14). On monthly charts "we can use the time period of 6 months or 12 months" (FSSDF 00:15:32). On daily charts "either you can use a 30 time period Or you can use 61 or 62, or some people use 123 as well So 123, 60 or 30, these are the 3 which I personally prefer" (FSSDF 00:17:45).
- RS (comparative, price-vs-benchmark) and RSI (momentum oscillator, price-vs-its-own-history) are unrelated tools that are commonly confused: "RSI has nothing to do with RS, both are operated in different dimensions and different galaxies" (FSSDF 00:23:51).
- The strongest entry read combines both: weekly RSI crossing above 50 together with RS turning positive against the chosen benchmark — "RSI crossing 50 and relative strength becoming green is a very very powerful signal" (FSSDF 00:26:39), reiterated as "a very very powerful signal for entry strategies" (FSSDF 00:27:42).
- A second, independent branch of the lecture is "fundamental relative strength" — comparing a company against its direct peers on operating metrics rather than the market: "We compare sales growth of peers, we compare capacity of peers, we compare margins of peers, we compare cash flow of peers, we use stage analysis for different sectors, we look at relative valuations and finally we look at return ratios like ROC, ROIC and ROE." (FSSDF 00:42:30) — this is presented as a screening confirmation layer that sits alongside, not instead of, price-based RS.

## 4. WHAT THE LADDER MISSES

(a) `entry_rsi-001` / `entry_adx-001` (gate G8) encode weekly RSI >= 50 AND weekly ADX >= 20, sourced from a different lecture (HOWB). This lecture describes what it calls its most powerful entry signal as weekly RSI crossing 50 combined with relative-strength-vs-benchmark turning positive — "RSI crossing 50 and relative strength becoming green is a very very powerful signal" (FSSDF 00:26:39) — not RSI+ADX. The ladder has no rule at all for the RS-vs-benchmark leg of this instructor's stated preferred setup; it substitutes a different second condition (ADX) that this lecture never mentions. This is case (b): a central mechanism (benchmark-relative price strength) the ladder has no rule for, sitting right next to a rule (entry_adx-001) whose second-condition choice this lecture does not corroborate.

(b) The entire "fundamental relative strength" (peer-to-peer) screening layer — comparing a company's sales growth, margins, ROC, and cash-flow quality against its direct named peers rather than against a fixed universal bar — has no analog anywhere in the 16-entry rulebook. Every existing rule and observation (canslim_sales, canslim_pat, capital_efficiency, cash_conversion, peg_ratio, etc.) is an absolute threshold applied uniformly across the whole 38-name universe. This lecture's stated method is explicitly the opposite: judge a company only against its own named industry peers (Polycab vs Finolex vs KEI vs Apar; SRF vs Neogen; Garware Hi-Tech vs Polyplex vs Cosmo). The ladder has no mechanism to group companies into peer sets and compare within them.

(c) The RSI-overbought caution (FSSDF 00:29:08, "cannot be a reason to particularly sell a stock") does not directly qualify any existing rule — the rulebook has no RSI>70/exit rule to begin with — so there is nothing here for it to condition. Flagging this only so it is not mistaken for a missed conflict: it is a warning against a rule the ladder does not have, not evidence against one it does.

Nothing here rises to case (c) (a dated one-company threshold mistaken for a universal bar) beyond what is already flagged above as data-availability gaps — the 13/26-week and 6/12-month and 30/62/123-period lookback numbers are all stated as the instructor's own general preferences ("these are the 3 which I personally prefer," FSSDF 00:17:45), not single-company examples, so they would generalize fine if the ladder ever gained benchmark-index price data.
